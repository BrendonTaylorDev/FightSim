— Nocturne —

The smell reached her first: wet otter fur, river-rank and warm, and under it the sharp, sour sweat of a body that had been hurt too often in too short a time. Nocturne's nostrils flared at it.

The otter had straightened from her crouch, but not all the way. She had stopped turning at the waist. When she had to look to one side she moved her whole body round on her feet, stiffly, rather than twist, because twisting made her gasp: Nocturne had heard it twice. One forearm stayed pressed low across the cream belly, and the other hand was held curled against the chest.

Something passed through Nocturne then, small and quick, like regret. She let it go. A trickle of grit sifted down from the top of the nearest leaning pillar and pattered on the stone.

She did not step closer. She kept the distance she had, a little more than she needed, and found she was keeping it out of something like respect.

*Stubborn little thing. All right.*

She planted herself low and wide on all four paws, the frost-stiff hind leg braced, and reached for what she had been storing. It was there, under her skin, packed tight along her spine. She let it come. The fur rose all over her in a slow wave, from the tuft on her head down her back to the root of the sickle tail, and the white ruff stood out round her neck like a mane. A thin crackle ran along her coat. The horn grew hot at its root and then hotter, and the dark blade of it began to glow, pale and humming, along its whole curved edge.

Her breath stopped while she aimed.

Then she opened her lungs and let all of it go out of her at once, every bit, through the horn. Thunderbolt.

— Ripples —

It did not hit her. It went into her.

There was a crackling sizzle in the air, and then the light was inside her, everywhere at once, from her ears to the soles of her feet, through her arms and her tails and the roots of every hair. It slipped past every way she had of bracing, the way water gets under a door. Her body answered it wrongly, stiffening where it should have given and giving where it should have held. Her twin tails jerked out in two different directions and dropped back behind her in a ragged bunch.

Her chest took the worst of it, and her chest was the worst place it could have gone.

The shock found all the old hurt there, the bruise of the charge and the scored lines of the claws and the tooth marks, and lit every one of them at once. Her chest caved in round it and her shoulders rounded, and a shriek tore up out of her, *kyaaa*, so high it cracked against the pillars. Every hurt in there at once, more than she could hold in. More than anything could.

And then her chest went out.

Not the pain easing. Gone, all at once, as if someone had laid a slab of cold wet stone where her chest had been. She could feel the edges of it, a dead heavy strangeness, and a cold shock spreading out round it into her shoulders, and in the middle nothing at all. She did not trust it for a heartbeat.

The rest of her went on burning. Her stomach jolted with it, the shriek catching and going higher, and she folded round the old claw lines there as if she could close over them. In her right paw a squeal broke and stopped short, *eek-*, and she flung the paw out and shook it hard to get the sting out of it. Her left arm jerked out sideways and a cry got out before she could stop it, her knees dipping under her. Behind her eyes there was a hard bright knock, and she blinked and blinked and they ran; her head ducked away from it and her left ear twitched and twitched, and she spat out an indignant *tch-tch* at it, absurdly, through her teeth.

The hair along her belly stood up in a crackling halo.

— Ripples —

She stood. She did not know how, but she was still standing.

The stomach came back to her first, out of the noise, and it came back too hot and too tight and far too loud to ignore. There was a deep pulse in there now, under the old claw marks, the place the dark claws had raked at the very beginning of everything, and the new hurt sat down inside the old one and made it bigger. Every so often, with no warning, it spiked.

She looked down. Her belly fur was standing straight out, fluffed and prickling, and under it her breath was coming in short, shaky pulls she could see.

Her throat worked. She swallowed, and swallowed again, chin tucked hard down over it out of habit. A hoarse sound kept trying to climb up out of her and she held it at the bottom of her throat.

Her right paw would not close. She tried. The webbed fingers curled halfway and shook there, and stopped, and she drew it back against her chest where she couldn't feel anything and held it there.

She dragged a breath down into herself as if it were heavy. Then another. Her mouth hung open with it; she was too tired to care how it sounded.

*Okay. Okay. That one hurt.*

Her chest felt like somebody else's. She did not want to think about when it would be hers again.

— Nocturne —

The light went out of her horn and the crackle died out of her coat, and her fur settled slowly down along her back. She stood very still.

Her own breath was loud. She slowed it so she could listen to the otter's: little high catches, each one swallowed before it could become a sound. Nocturne's ears turned to them one by one.

The otter's knees kept bending under her, a little at a time, and each time she locked them straight again. The shaking had started in the right paw and was everywhere now, in her elbows, in the stiff hanging tails, in the black marks on her cheeks.

At one high thin note Nocturne felt a sting of something like guilt. It was gone before it slowed her.

*Still up. Then show me what's left.*

She was breathing hard herself. The stored charge was spent and it had taken something with it, a hollowness behind her breastbone. Her right ribs throbbed where she had dragged all the air out past them, and the left hind paw was still stiff and cold where the frost had got in. She had not felt either while the lightning was running. She felt them now. For the moment they were quiet.
