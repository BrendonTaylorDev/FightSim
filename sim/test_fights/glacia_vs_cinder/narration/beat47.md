— Cinder —

Cinder took a step back from the bird, and then another. She did not take her eye off the still blue shape on the stone. It was a foolish thing, she knew; the bird had not so much as twitched. But she had watched that bird get up off this rock too many times today, and some part of her went on waiting for it.

Nothing moved.

Her legs did not want to hold her. Each step back had been a decision, and the second one had nearly not happened. She stopped where she was and stood, swaying a little, her claws gripping the grit. If she sat down now she was not sure she would get up again, so she did not sit.

Winning had taken nearly everything she had. She could feel exactly how much was left, and it was very little: a heaviness in every limb, a burning in her throat on each breath, a dull throb behind the swollen eye that had not eased at all. Her left wing hung half open at her side. The wind tugged at it, and each tug went through the root of it like a hand dragging on a bruise. Her belly had gone stiff and cold.

She thought, for no reason, of the bird hanging in the wind over the shelf, flinging that last shard of ice at her face on wings that could barely hold her. And of the kick, at the very end, when she had thought there was nothing left in her at all.

The pride came then. It came late, and it was smaller than she had expected: not a roar, only a slow warmth somewhere under her ribs. She had beaten a strong one. It had cost her almost everything she had.

Her tail flame lifted a little, a finger's width, and settled again.

— Glacia —

The bird lay as she had lain, on her back on the cold rock, wings spread, eyes shut.

Her beak had fallen slightly open. Breath went in and out through it, slow and shallow, a faint whisper of air and a fainter thread of mist. Her throat moved with it under the bruised and crumpled feathers.

Once, the very tip of her long tail twitched against the stone, a small, dry scrape of feathers on grit, and lay still again. Nothing else followed it. Her eyes did not open. Her talons stayed loose and half curled on the rock.

The light had shifted while they lay there. The sun had moved out over the cloud, and the shadow of the cliff had crept a little way across the shelf toward her. Its edge lay near her outflung wing now, a cool grey line on the stone, not quite touching.

Up here the wind never stopped. It went on pouring across the shelf and over the edge, lifting a few loose feathers along her wing, flattening them again, and the cloud below rolled slowly on, white and endless.

She lay still in the middle of it, breathing.
