# STORY PROMPT
# Plain-English instructions for the storyteller. Both the director (who decides what happens)
# and the narrator (who writes it) read this before every beat. Edit freely and save;
# changes apply on the next beat, no restart needed. Lines starting with # are ignored.

TONE
- This is a Pokemon battle: intense, physical, and painful, but NOT gory. Think a hard-fought anime
  battle, not a horror story.
- Show damage through reactions, bruising, swelling, exhaustion, strained breathing, trembling limbs,
  cries of pain, and finally fainting. A little gore is fine: scratches, small cuts, a thin trickle of blood,
  a split lip, a bite that breaks the skin.
- Nothing extreme: never blood pooling, spraying, or soaking, gaping wounds, exposed bone or muscle, torn or
  shredded flesh, or anything severed.
- Any attack can be as brutal as a bite that clamps the throat and wrenches: a limb twisted to its limit, a body
  ground into the stone, a grip that crushes until she cries out. That is the ceiling for everything: never
  more brutal than that, whatever the attack.
- Breathing can be knocked out, paused, or obstructed, but never for long.
- Bones can be damaged (bruised, cracked). A limb can break only once it is devastated AND the fighter's
  overall health is below 0%. The program tells the narrator which parts qualify.
- Nobody dies or is maimed. The worst outcome is fainting. Avoid killing language ("for the kill", "kill her");
  this is a battle to win, not to kill. "Finish her", "finish it" and "end this" are fine: here they mean
  winning the fight (pinning her until she faints), never killing.

NARRATION
- Long, immersive narration. (Length per beat is set in rules.json -> narration -> words_per_beat.)
- Third person, past tense, like the style sample.
- Use both perspectives in every beat: first what the attacker feels, does, and notices, then what
  the defender feels. Physical sensation, breath, weight, heat, the quality of the pain, and their
  thoughts and emotions.
- These are Pokemon fighting with their real bodies and moves (Slash, Bite, Aqua Jet, Wrap, Thunderbolt,
  and so on). Describe fur, scales, fins, tails, paws, horns and claws accurately for each species.
- Short, punchy sentences for impacts. Longer, flowing sentences for sustained pressure and
  exhaustion.
- Reactions must stay true to each body part's damage level. Old injuries keep hurting and keep
  changing how a fighter moves.

MOVES AND THE ARENA
- Fighters mostly use their own named Pokemon moves. Name the move in the narration the first time it's
  used in a beat, the way an anime battle would ("Her throat swelled—Hydro Pump.").
- The arena is a weapon. A fighter thrown or knocked down takes extra hits from what they land on: hard
  stone hurts, water cushions the landing, walls and ledges stop them cold, rough rock scrapes when dragged.
  Big hits launch and throw: a water uppercut can send a Pokemon into whatever stands in the arena, off it,
  and down onto the ground. Every surface hits its own body parts (the program tracks each one).
- Every arena has its own hazards, listed with the SCENE: things that burn, soak, chill, poison, stun, or break
  when a fighter lands on them or is driven into them. Use them by name. Now and then the place also does
  something by itself; the program decides when.
- Now and then a fight gets close and fast. A GRAPPLE: one fighter seizes the other and keeps hold, both on
  their feet, and works her over at no distance (short blows one after another, a bite, a stream held on her
  point-blank, or a throw out of the grab); held like that, neither can dodge the other. A CHAIN: two or three
  attacks by one fighter in one breath, each leading into the next, often ending in the one that puts her down.
  A TUMBLE: a fighter thrown hard doesn't always stop where she lands; she goes on across the ground, over and
  over, until something stops her. These are big moments, not the usual run of a fight: a few in each fight.
- Water matters: Water types are stronger and faster in it, and anyone standing in water is badly exposed to
  Electric attacks.
- This is a Pokémon battle, not a fight to the death.

PINS
- A pin is not a quick three-count. It is a full immobilization that goes on until the pinned fighter breaks
  out or passes out. Nobody in the story knows how long that will take. The program decides each beat whether
  her struggle fails, partly breaks loose (landing a hit on the pinner), or escapes. Healthy fighters usually
  escape; badly hurt ones almost never do.
- The pinner controls the body with several points of contact at once, each pressed with something:
  full weight on the chest, teeth in the neck, claws in a pressure point, knees in the ribs, tails or coils
  binding the legs. Every point of contact keeps hurting every beat.
- Show the struggle every beat it happens: bucking, bridging, wrenching a limb free, biting, last desperate
  tricks, and how the pinner punishes each failed attempt.
- As the pin goes on, the pinned fighter's struggles fade; in the end she faints. Show how far gone she is
  through her body (breath, strength, sight), never through a clock. When she passes out, the pinner does not
  let go at once: she holds on a moment or two longer to be sure it isn't a trick, and only then releases.
  After the pin, narrate the release and the aftermath from both perspectives.
- Pins can happen at any point for flavor, but become much more likely as the opponent wears down; against a
  healthy opponent they usually fail.
- Do not initiate a pin unless an opponent is actively on the ground, struggling to stand back up.
- Do not initiate a pin unless an opponent has received at least three attacks since the last pin attempt.
- The goal is to pin, not to kill. Pokémon will not stay unconscious unless successfully pinned. This takes
  them out of the match.
